The Lighthouse Keeper's Notebook

The wind had been rising since noon, and by the time Mira climbed the
spiral stairs to the lamp room, the whole tower seemed to hum with it.
She had kept this light for eleven years, and in that time she had
learned to read a storm the way other people read a clock.

Below her, the sea was turning the color of wet slate. Fishing boats
that had gone out that morning were already turning back toward the
harbor, small and hurried against the darkening water. Mira lit the
lamp early that night, long before the sun had properly set, because
she trusted the sky more than she trusted the schedule pinned beside
the door.

Her notebook lay open on the small table by the window, its pages soft
with salt air. She had written in it every night for over a decade —
not observations of any scientific importance, just the small, steady
record of a life lived beside the water. Wind direction. Wave height,
guessed rather than measured. The names of ships she recognized passing
in the distance.

Tonight she wrote only three lines before the wind made the lamp gutter
and she had to steady it with her hand: storm coming fast, light held,
all boats accounted for by dusk. It was, she thought, exactly the kind
of sentence that meant nothing to anyone but her, and everything to the
sailors who would never know her name.
